Gemini 4 Argon: What Google's New Model Actually Means for You

Google announced Gemini 4 Argon on September 30. If you opened the Gemini app afterward hoping to try it, you didn't find it. That's not a bug. Right now Argon is only available to trusted testers and a group of cybersecurity partners in Google's Fairwind Program. Everyone else is still on Gemini 3.8 Flash.

So this isn't a hands-on review. I haven't tested Argon, and almost nobody outside Google has. What I can do is go through what Google actually announced, separate the numbers that matter from the marketing, and tell you whether it's worth paying attention to.

First, the Name

Google took a detour to get here. Gemini 3.5 Pro was cancelled over the summer, and the company spent the past few months shipping one Flash model after another: 3.5, 3.6, 3.7 and 3.8, all between May and September. Argon is Google's first new flagship since Gemini 3.1 Pro in February. It also starts a new naming scheme, a version number plus a name, which looks a lot like what OpenAI is doing with GPT-6 Astra, Sol and Luna.

What's Actually New

The headline spec is output length. Argon can generate up to 1 million tokens in a single response, up from 64,000. If you mostly type questions into a chat box, that number means nothing to you. You will never ask for a million-token answer. It matters for long, autonomous work: a model that can keep reasoning and writing code for hours without hitting a ceiling. Google's pitch is basically "give it a hard problem and let it think."

Google is focusing on four areas: coding, knowledge work, cybersecurity defense and creative writing. Cybersecurity gets the most attention. Google says Argon can find, verify and patch serious software vulnerabilities on its own, and approved security teams will get a version without the usual cyber guardrails.

The Part That Wasn't in the Press Release

On launch day, Bloomberg reported that some people inside Google are skeptical of how well Argon performs in key areas, including coding. That's an awkward fit with a launch that leads with a coding benchmark. It doesn't mean Google's numbers are wrong. Benchmarks and everyday use often disagree, and that's exactly why I run my own side-by-side tests on this site instead of just repeating scores.

What Google Says Argon Is Already Doing

The more interesting part of the announcement isn't the benchmark table. It's what Google says Argon has already done inside the company:

  • Teams of Argon agents analyzed memory usage across Google's data centers and applied optimizations that freed up more than 300 TiB of memory.
  • Argon agents are rewriting C and C++ code into Rust, from small libraries up to an 800,000-line operating system kernel.
  • For libgav1, Google's open-source video decoder, Argon replaced 32,000 lines of low-level code. The result runs 2.7x faster than the previous Rust version, with identical video output.

These are Google's claims about its own work, so read them with that in mind. But they're far more concrete than "state-of-the-art reasoning."

Price: The Detail I'd Watch

At launch, Argon's API will cost $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens. That's an introductory price. Once it ends, the price doubles to $4 and $20.

Here's what stood out to me: $2 and $10 is exactly what OpenAI charges for GPT-6 Sol, its mid-tier model, not its flagship. GPT-6 Astra costs $50 per million output tokens. If Argon is anywhere near as good as Google says, that intro price is aggressive. The catch is the word "introductory."

For regular users, Google hasn't said anything about the free plan. Argon will roll out first to Google AI Ultra subscribers and paid API customers.

So Should You Care?

  • If you use Gemini for free: Nothing changes today. You're on 3.8 Flash, which is still a solid model for everyday tasks.
  • If you pay for Google AI Ultra: You'll probably be first in line. Watch for Argon in the model picker.
  • If you're a developer: The intro pricing is the real story. If you want to test it, do it before the price doubles.
  • If you're happy with ChatGPT or Claude: This announcement alone isn't a reason to switch. Wait for real-world results. If you're shopping around anyway, see our ChatGPT alternatives guide.

What's Next

When Argon is available to regular users, I'll run it through the same tests I've done with ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini: same prompts, real screenshots, results checked by hand. Until then, treat every number in this post as Google's claim, not a verdict.
